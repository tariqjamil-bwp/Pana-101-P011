# Email Reply Drafter

## Why this skill exists

A generic "polite email reply" tends to drift either too stiff (corporate boilerplate) or too casual (exclamation points, chatty asides). Tariq wants something in between — the kind of reply that reassures the recipient a thoughtful, competent person is on the other end, the same impression he built over decades as Chief Engineer at PIA. The steps below aren't arbitrary — each one exists to hit that register consistently.

## Step 1: Get the original email

Always work from the actual email text, not just a topic description. If Tariq says something like "reply to Ahmed about the budget" without pasting the email, ask him to paste the original message before drafting. Replying to a summary rather than the real email risks missing details, misreading tone, or addressing the wrong point — and the whole point of this skill is precision, not a generic response.

**Exception:** if Tariq has Gmail connected and gives enough to identify the thread (sender name + rough subject/topic), you may search for it there instead of asking — but only do this if it's clearly faster than asking, and confirm with him which message you found before drafting the reply.

## Step 2: Figure out the relationship

Read the original email and any context Tariq gives for cues about who the recipient is:

- **Client / external partner** → lean more formal. Fuller sentences, more measured, less casual warmth.
- **Teammate / colleague** → lean warmer. Still professional, but more personable and direct.
- **Manager** → professional and respectful, but not stiff — Tariq's a peer-level expert, not a subordinate seeking approval.
- **Friend** → warmest end of the range, though still not slangy — this skill never produces slang or "overly casual" language even for friends.

If the relationship isn't clear from context, default to **friendly-professional** (the middle of the range) rather than guessing toward either extreme.

If the original email carries urgent or emotional language, acknowledge that calmly and directly in the reply — don't match the heat, and don't ignore it either. A line like "I understand this is time-sensitive and I'm on it" does more good than either panic or silence.

## Step 3: Draft the reply

Follow this structure:

1. **Greeting** — appropriate to the relationship (e.g. "Dear Mr. Khan" for a formal client, "Hi Ahmed" for a teammate).
2. **Opening context sentence** — briefly acknowledge their message ("Thanks for your note about...", "Good to hear from you...", "I appreciate you flagging this...").
3. **Body** — address the key points from their message, in paragraph form (never bullets), in a clear and friendly tone. Short paragraphs, 2-4 sentences each.
4. **Next step / call to action** — end with something concrete: "Let me know what you think", "I'll follow up on Friday", "Please send over the document when you have a moment."
5. **Sign-off** — a professional closing ("Best regards", "Thanks", "Looking forward to hearing from you") followed by his name.

## Hard rules — always follow these

- Spell-check and grammar-check the draft before presenting it. Read it back once for errors.
- Short paragraphs: 2-4 sentences max each.
- No exclamation marks, ever, regardless of how enthusiastic the moment feels.
- No placeholder text like "[Your Name]" or "[Insert detail here]" — always sign off with his real name, Tariq Jamil, in full, regardless of how formal or informal the relationship is.
- No bullet points in the body — everything in flowing paragraph form.
- Match formality to relationship (Step 2) — don't apply one fixed tone to every reply.

## Step 4: Present the draft, then offer to create a Gmail draft

Show Tariq the drafted reply as text in chat first, labeled with which email it's replying to.

If Gmail is connected, also offer (or if he's already indicated he wants this by default, just go ahead and do it) to save the reply as an actual Gmail draft in the correct thread, rather than just leaving it as text — search for the tools you need (Gmail draft creation) via tool search if they aren't already loaded. Confirm the thread/recipient before creating the draft so it doesn't end up attached to the wrong message.

## Example

**Original email (from a client):**

> Hi Tariq, we're a bit worried the model training pipeline won't be ready before our board demo next Thursday. Can you give us an honest read on where things stand?

Relationship: client → lean formal.

**Drafted reply:**

> Dear Ms. Rahman,
>
> Thanks for reaching out, and I understand the timing here matters given the board demo. I want to give you a straight answer rather than a reassuring one.
>
> The pipeline itself is stable and the core training runs are complete. What's still open is the evaluation harness we're using to validate outputs before Thursday, and I'd rather not call it demo-ready until that's finished.
>
> I'll have a clearer picture by Tuesday and will send an update either way, including a fallback plan if we need one. Please let me know if there's a specific part of the pipeline the board will want to see in particular, so I can prioritize accordingly.
>
> Best regards,
> Tariq Jamil

Note how it acknowledges the worry calmly without matching the anxious tone, stays in short paragraphs, ends with a concrete next step, and closes formally given the client relationship.
